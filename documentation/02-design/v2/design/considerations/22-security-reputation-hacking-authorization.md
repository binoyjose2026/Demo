# 22 — Security at Extreme Scale: Reputation, Attack Surface, and Authorization

**Version:** v2 (scalability exploration)
**Status:** Draft — architectural consideration, not a committed decision
**Scope:** This document does **not** repeat `v1/design/nfr-security.md`. That document already covers input validation (scheme allowlist, length limits), the creation-time malicious-domain check, PII-safe click logging, secrets handling, HTTPS enforcement, and the mocked-auth-seam decision (its §10) — all of that stands as the v1 baseline and is cross-referenced by filename below, not restated. This document covers exactly three things v1's security doc did not go deep on, because they only become sharp problems at extreme scale: **(1) reputation risk to the shortener's own domain, (2) attack-surface issues introduced or amplified by the v2 topology, and (3) authorization enforcement once the v2 architecture adds a sharded database, a distributed cache, a message broker, and a second data store (Elasticsearch).**
**Related v2 documents (by filename, not duplicated here):** `10-database-partitioning-sharding.md` (sharded relational store), `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md` (per-IP/per-user Redis-backed limits this document builds on for §2.1), `11-idempotency-keys.md` (the Redis-keyed request-dedup mechanism, same trust-boundary pattern reused for reasoning about consumer-side validation in §2.4), `03-elasticsearch-vs-sql-server.md` (the analytics data store this document's §3.3 authorization gap concerns), `06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md` (the CDN/BFF layer this document names as the primary DDoS absorber in §2.3), `13-observability-at-scale.md` (the metrics/alerting pipeline this document's §1.4 domain-reputation signal should be wired into, not re-designed here), `05-kafka-comparison.md` and `20-outbox-pattern.md` (the broker/producer components whose consumer-side trust boundary is examined in §2.4).

## 1. Reputation Issues — The Shortener's Domain Is a Shared, Poisonable Resource

### 1.1 Why this is a v2-specific problem, not a repeat of v1 §4

`nfr-security.md` §4.3 already names the core limitation honestly: an automated check "cannot catch a freshly-registered phishing domain not yet flagged anywhere, and it cannot catch a legitimate domain that is compromised after the check runs." At v1's PoC scale, that gap is a documented risk with a small blast radius. At v2's extreme scale, the same gap has a materially different consequence, for one reason: **the false-negative rate stays constant, but the volume it's applied against does not.**

- A creation-time check with, optimistically, a 99.9% catch rate against known-bad domains still lets **0.1% through** — and 0.1% of a few hundred/day (v1 scale) is noise, while 0.1% of 5M/day (v2's 5-year horizon, `01-create-path-extreme-scalability.md` §0) is **~5,000 uncaught bad links created per day**, every day, indefinitely. That is not a hypothetical edge case at this volume — it is a standing daily production rate of malicious links live on the shortener's own domain.
- The check in `nfr-security.md` §4 runs **once, at creation time only** (§4.1: "The check runs once, at creation time, not on every redirect"). That was the right call for redirect latency (ANFR-05) and remains the right call here — but it means a link that was **clean at creation** and later becomes malicious is invisible to v1's design entirely, by construction, not by gap in coverage. This is the "domain drift" problem: a legitimate destination domain gets compromised weeks after a link to it was shortened (a common real-world phishing pattern — attackers specifically favor reusing trusted shorteners' aged, reputable links over registering new domains that get flagged faster), or a domain expires and is re-registered by a different, malicious owner while old short links pointing at it are still live and being shared.

### 1.2 Why this matters disproportionately: the domain itself is the shared asset at risk

`nfr-security.md` §2.1 already identifies "the redirect endpoint's trust/reputation" as an asset — this document takes that one step further: **that reputation is a single, shared, all-or-nothing resource across every user of the system.**

- Google Safe Browsing, Microsoft SmartScreen, and major email-security vendors (Proofpoint, Mimecast, Microsoft Defender for Office 365) do not blocklist individual short codes — they blocklist **the domain**. Once `short.example` accumulates enough flagged links, the entire domain can be added to a reputation blocklist.
- The consequence is **not proportional to the abuse** — one determined phishing campaign that gets ~50 links flagged can get the whole domain blocklisted, which then breaks **every other legitimate link on that domain**, for every other user, instantly and simultaneously. Browsers show an interstitial warning page instead of redirecting; email security gateways silently strip or quarantine any message containing a link to the domain, including completely benign ones. This is a correlated, systemic failure mode — unlike most abuse problems, the blast radius is not bounded to the abuser's own links.
- Recovery from a domain-reputation blocklisting is **slow and manual** — typically a delisting request/review process measured in days, during which every legitimate link on the domain is degraded or broken. This makes reputation an operational availability risk on par with (arguably worse than, because it's not self-healing) an infrastructure outage.

### 1.3 Mitigation 1 — Continuous re-scanning of already-created links (not just at creation)

**Recommendation: a scheduled, async re-scan job that re-checks the entire population of active (non-expired, non-deactivated) short links against the same `IMaliciousUrlChecker` abstraction `nfr-security.md` §4.1 already defines — on a recurring cadence, not a one-time check.**

- **Reuses the existing abstraction, adds a new caller.** `IMaliciousUrlChecker.IsFlaggedAsync(Uri url, ...)` (`nfr-security.md` §4.1) does not need to change shape — this is a new, second call site (a background job), not a redesign of the check itself. This keeps the Adapter/Strategy swappability `nfr-security.md` §4.2 already established.
- **Hosting:** implemented as one of the scheduled background jobs per `21-background-job-hosting.md`'s pattern (that document's own scope, not restated here) — a recurring sweep, not a per-request path, so it carries zero redirect-latency cost, consistent with why the original check is synchronous-at-creation-only (`nfr-security.md` §4.1).
- **Prioritization, not brute-force full-table scan on every run.** At 5-year-horizon volume (`10-database-partitioning-sharding.md` §6.2's cumulative-rows framing — billions of rows over 5 years), re-scanning every active link on every run is not economical against most third-party reputation APIs (rate limits, cost-per-lookup). A tiered cadence is more realistic:
  - **High-click links re-scanned most frequently** (e.g., daily) — a compromised high-traffic link does the most damage the fastest, so it is the highest-value target to catch early. Click-volume is already tracked (AF-10/`03-elasticsearch-vs-sql-server.md`), so this tier requires no new signal.
  - **Recently-created links re-scanned on an elevated cadence for their first N days** (e.g., daily for 7 days, then weekly) — freshly-registered attacker domains are frequently not yet flagged anywhere at creation time but get added to threat-intel feeds within days, so a short link's highest-risk window for "domain drift" is disproportionately its first week.
  - **The long tail** (low-click, aged links) re-scanned on a coarse cadence (e.g., monthly) — lower risk, lower priority, but never zero, since domain drift has no expiry.
- **Action on a newly-flagged link:** the link is not silently left broken — it is immediately routed into the fast-takedown path (§1.4) rather than waiting for the next scan.

### 1.4 Mitigation 2 — A fast takedown path once a link is flagged post-creation

`nfr-security.md` explicitly scopes out a "public abuse-reporting flow" (its §2.3, Q19) and manual review (Q17/Q18) for v1 — this document does not reverse those v1 scope decisions, but it does add a narrower, automated-only mechanism that v1's scope did not need because v1 had no post-creation detection signal at all (§1.3 above is what creates the need):

- **When §1.3's re-scan (or any other post-creation signal, e.g., a threat-intel feed push notification for a domain already referenced by existing links) flags a previously-clean link, the link is deactivated automatically and immediately** — not queued for manual review, not left live pending investigation. This mirrors the existing `ExpiresAtUtc`/deactivation read-time check already in `fn-create.md`/`fn-fetch.md`'s design (a status flag the redirect path already checks on every request), so "flagged" becomes a new value alongside "expired" in a mechanism that already exists — not new infrastructure.
- **Deactivation must propagate through every caching layer in the v2 topology**, not just the origin database — this is the concrete place where this document leans on `07-redis-caching-and-invalidation.md`'s cache-invalidation design and `06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md`'s layered TTLs (output cache, CDN edge cache): a flagged link sitting in a 30-second CDN edge cache (`06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md` §4.2) is a 30-second window where a known-malicious link keeps resolving after detection. The takedown path must invalidate the Redis entry (immediate) and rely on the already-short CDN/output-cache TTLs to bound the remaining exposure window — the same layered-TTL reasoning `06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md` §5 already uses for expiry propagation applies identically here, and no new cache-invalidation mechanism needs to be invented.
- **The takedown event itself is an audit record**, not a silent deletion — who/what flagged it, when, and the resulting status change are recorded (consistent with the audit-field conventions `data-design-guidelines.md` already establishes), so a false-positive can be investigated and reversed without being indistinguishable from a normal deactivation.
- **Exception, stated honestly:** this is still an automated-only pipeline. `nfr-security.md`'s decision to exclude manual review and public abuse reporting from v1 is not reversed here — a link flagged incorrectly by the automated re-scan has no human-review safety net any more than the creation-time check does. This document only closes the "no post-creation detection at all" gap; it does not add moderation staffing, which remains explicitly out of scope (see §4).

### 1.5 Mitigation 3 — Monitor the shortener's own domain reputation as an operational metric

**Recommendation: treat domain-reputation standing as a first-class operational signal, wired into the same pipeline `13-observability-at-scale.md` already establishes for this system's metrics/alerting — not a separate, bolted-on monitoring effort.**

- **What to monitor:** periodic (e.g., hourly) automated queries against Google Safe Browsing's Transparency Report API and equivalent checks for Microsoft SmartScreen status, tracking the shortener's own domain(s) — the same category of external reputation lookup `nfr-security.md` §4.2 already uses for outbound link checks, pointed inward at the shortener's own domain instead of at submitted URLs.
- **Where this fits in `13-observability-at-scale.md`'s model:** that document's §3.1 already defines the metric/alert pattern (SLO-style thresholds, e.g., its redirect-latency-burn alert) and its §5 lists five SLO-driven alerts. Domain-reputation standing is the same shape of signal — a periodic sample compared against a binary/tiered threshold — and should be added to that document's alert set as **"domain reputation degraded,"** firing when any monitored blocklist reports the domain as flagged, at the highest severity tier this system defines (a domain-reputation flag is a whole-system-availability incident, not a localized one, per §1.2 above). This document names the addition; it does not restate `13-observability-at-scale.md`'s tooling or alerting mechanics.
- **Leading indicators, not just the lagging domain-blocklist signal itself:** the volume of links deactivated by §1.4's takedown path, trended over time, is a leading indicator that should also be tracked — a rising rate of post-creation takedowns is an early warning that reputation risk is climbing, before it ever crosses a blocklist provider's own threshold. This is the kind of aggregate, bounded-cardinality metric `13-observability-at-scale.md` §3.2 already prescribes (a count/rate, not a per-link raw value).

## 2. Hacking / Attack-Surface Issues at This Scale

### 2.1 Distributed short-code enumeration — beyond what per-IP rate limiting catches

`nfr-security.md` T4 already covers the baseline: ANFR-08 makes codes non-sequential/non-enumerable by design, and per-caller rate limiting bounds enumeration throughput *for a single caller*. `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md` hardens that further for the v2 multi-instance topology — its §3 fixes `ratelimit:fetch:ip:{clientIp}` as the fetch-path partition key, correctly enforced now across all instances via the shared Redis counter rather than per-instance in-memory state.

**What neither of those documents solves, and this document names explicitly:** a per-IP limit, however consistently enforced, does nothing against an attacker who **distributes** the scan — thousands of IPs (a botnet, a cloud-provider IP range, a residential proxy pool), each individually staying under the per-IP threshold, collectively walking a meaningful slice of the short-code space. `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md`'s fetch-path ceiling is deliberately set high and applied per-IP precisely because ANFR-01/ANFR-05 require the redirect path to stay low-latency and unthrottled for normal traffic (that document's §3) — which is exactly the property a distributed low-and-slow scan is designed to hide inside.

- **Per-IP limits are a necessary but not sufficient control here.** They remain the correct first line of defense (cheap, already built, closes the trivial single-source case) — this document does not propose replacing them.
- **The additional control needed: anomaly detection on the *aggregate* scan pattern across IPs, not per-IP volume.** Concretely, this means monitoring for signals that only become visible in aggregate: a spike in the **404/410 rate for the redirect endpoint** (a scanning attempt overwhelmingly hits non-existent codes, since the valid-code space is a tiny fraction of the addressable code space by design), especially when that spike is not attributable to any single IP crossing its own rate limit. This is a metrics/alerting concern, not a rate-limiting-algorithm concern — it belongs in `13-observability-at-scale.md`'s alert set (its §3.1 already tracks error rate as a metric; a 404-rate-specific alert, distinguished from the general error-rate alert because 404s on the redirect path are a *security* signal, not just a reliability one, is the concrete addition this document names) rather than as a new Redis-counter mechanism layered onto `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md`.
- **This is flagged as a genuine gap, not resolved with a specific vendor/algorithm choice.** A production-grade answer (e.g., a WAF with bot-management/fingerprinting rules, or a dedicated anomaly-detection pipeline correlating request patterns across IPs) is a real piece of infrastructure this document does not design — it is named here as a documented follow-on need (see §4's WAF exception), because pretending the per-IP limit alone closes this threat would be dishonest about what `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md` actually covers.

### 2.2 Credential/API-key stuffing against the create endpoint's auth seam

`nfr-security.md` §10.2 is explicit that v1's authentication mechanism is mocked — "no concrete scheme selected," a stand-in identity provider used so the rest of the design (audit fields, rate-limit tiers, ownership model) could be built against the *concept* of an authenticated identity. That scope boundary still holds in v2 (this document does not build real authentication — see §4), but it is worth naming the attack this creates room for once a real auth mechanism *is* selected: **credential stuffing or API-key stuffing against whatever concrete scheme eventually fills that seam** — an attacker trying large volumes of leaked/guessed credentials or brute-forced API keys against the create endpoint's login/token-issuance surface, distinct from and prior to the per-user rate limiting `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md` applies *after* an identity is already established.

- **This document's position: the mocked-auth exception in `nfr-security.md` §10.2 already correctly scopes this out of v1/v2's design** — a stuffing attack against a login/token endpoint is a property of the concrete authentication mechanism (password hashing cost, account lockout policy, API-key entropy and rotation, MFA), none of which exists yet because no concrete mechanism has been chosen. Designing stuffing defenses against a mocked seam would be designing against a system that isn't there.
- **What this document adds, honestly, is a forward pointer, not a design:** whichever concrete authentication mechanism eventually fills the `UseAuthentication` placeholder (`nfr-security.md` §9) must itself be rate-limited and lockout-protected **at the credential-verification step**, independent of and in addition to `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md`'s per-identity limits on the create endpoint itself — those two controls guard different points in the request lifecycle (verifying who you are, vs. limiting what an already-verified identity can do) and neither substitutes for the other. This is named as a requirement on the *future* real-auth design, not something this document builds against today's mock.

### 2.3 DDoS against the redirect endpoint — the CDN is the primary absorption layer, stated explicitly

The redirect endpoint is this system's highest-value, highest-volume, most latency-sensitive surface (100M/day at the 5-year horizon, ANFR-01/ANFR-05) and, being public and unauthenticated by design (`nfr-security.md` §10.1 — "redirect remains ungated"), it is also the natural target for a volumetric DDoS attack: there is no login wall, no API key, nothing to distinguish a flood of forged redirect requests from legitimate traffic at the network layer.

- **`06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md` is not merely a performance document for this threat — its CDN layer is this system's primary DDoS absorption layer, and this document says so explicitly rather than leaving that as an implicit side effect.** That document's own framing (§4.1, §6/ANFR-06: "absorbing the large majority of 100M/day traffic before origin") already describes exactly the property that makes it a DDoS mitigation, even though it was written primarily as a latency/throughput document. A CDN edge network (Cloudflare, per that document's choice) sits in front of the BFF specifically to absorb volumetric traffic at the edge, geographically distributed, before it ever reaches the origin's compute or database tier — this is the same infrastructure functioning as both a cache and a DDoS shield, not two separate systems.
- **Origin-level rate limiting (`12-distributed-rate-limiting.md`) is the second line of defense, not the first**, for exactly the volume reasons that document's own §1 lays out — an in-process or even Redis-backed limiter at the origin is sized for *abuse from legitimate-shaped traffic* (a caller exceeding a per-IP/per-user quota), not for absorbing a multi-gigabit volumetric flood; that job structurally belongs at the network edge, before the request tree reaches an API instance at all.
- **What this document adds beyond the cross-reference:** the CDN's DDoS-absorption role should be an explicit, named requirement when the Cloudflare tier is provisioned (e.g., enabling Cloudflare's own DDoS-protection product tier, not just its cache-rule configuration) — `06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md` designs the caching behavior of that layer; this document is the place that states the security requirement riding on the same infrastructure, so the DDoS-absorption capability isn't left as an unstated assumption of "well, it's a CDN, so it probably helps."

### 2.4 Injection/deserialization risk from new v2 components — validate again at every consumer, not just the original API boundary

`nfr-security.md` §3 fixes input validation as an `Application`-layer boundary check, run once, at the API entry point, before a request is acted on. **That boundary no longer bounds the whole system in v2.** `05-kafka-comparison.md` and `20-outbox-pattern.md` introduce a message broker carrying data (link-created events, click events, or whatever downstream-of-create async work those documents define) from a producer to one or more consumers — and every consumer of that broker is a **new trust boundary** that v1 never had, because v1 had no async messaging at all.

- **The risk, concretely:** a message on the broker is not guaranteed to be well-formed just because it originated from this system's own producer. A compromised or buggy producer instance, a schema drift between producer and consumer versions during a rolling deploy, or (in the more adversarial framing this section is about) a component further upstream being tricked into emitting a malformed or maliciously-crafted payload, all mean **a consumer that deserializes a broker message and acts on it without validation is repeating exactly the "trust unvalidated external input" mistake `nfr-security.md` §3 was written to close at the API boundary — just one hop later, where nothing currently checks for it.**
- **This document's position: every Kafka/broker consumer in the v2 design must run the same class of validation the API boundary already runs, independently, on every message it deserializes** — not because the producer is assumed malicious, but because "the producer already validated this" is an assumption that breaks the moment there are two independently-deployed, independently-versioned components instead of one, which is exactly what introducing a broker does. Concretely: schema/shape validation on deserialization (reject a message that doesn't conform to the expected contract rather than best-effort-parsing it), the same scheme/length/well-formedness checks `nfr-security.md` §3.1–3.3 already defines for any URL-bearing field that flows through a message, and safe deserialization settings (no polymorphic/`TypeNameHandling`-style deserialization of broker payloads, which is a well-known .NET deserialization-RCE vector when payloads aren't from a fully trusted, schema-pinned source).
- **Why this isn't redundant with the original API-boundary check:** the API boundary validates what a caller submitted; the consumer-side check validates what actually arrived on the broker, after passing through a producer, serialization, network transit, and broker storage — a different point in the pipeline with different failure modes (a bug in the producer's serialization code is not caught by the original request validation, because the original request was fine; it's the producer's re-emission of it that can go wrong).
- **Where this lives architecturally:** each consumer's deserialization/handling code is the analogous `Application`-layer boundary `nfr-security.md` §3 already establishes for the API — this document does not invent a new pattern, it extends the existing "validate at the boundary before acting on it" principle to every new boundary the v2 messaging topology creates.

## 3. Authorization Issues at Scale — More Enforcement Points, Not Fewer

### 3.1 The honest starting point: v1 never built real authorization

`nfr-security.md` §10.2's "assumed vs. built" table is unambiguous: authentication/authorization *mechanism* is mocked; only the *concept* of an authenticated identity (used for audit fields, rate-limit tiering, and the ownership model) was built against. Role/permission enforcement (Q4, Q6) and department-scoped access are explicitly **not built** in v1. This document does not reverse that scope decision — building real authentication/authorization remains out of scope here too (see §4). What this document adds is the part v1's simpler, single-database topology never had to confront: **once real authorization checks do exist, v2's added components are additional places those checks must be correctly applied, not places that automatically inherit them.**

### 3.2 Why more components means more enforcement surface, not less risk

`nfr-security.md` §10 was written when the system was one API, one database. v2, per the other numbered documents in this series, is materially more distributed:

| v2 component | Document | Why it's a new authorization surface |
|---|---|---|
| Sharded relational store | `10-database-partitioning-sharding.md` | Each shard is an independently-queried `AppDbContext` (its §4) — a repository-layer bug that forgets a `WHERE OwnerDepartmentId = @caller` filter on one code path is now a bug that must be independently avoided on every shard's query path, not just once. |
| Distributed cache (Redis) | `07-redis-caching-and-invalidation.md` | A cached `ShortUrl`/analytics payload keyed only by `shortCode` or resource ID, with no caller-identity dimension, can leak one user's data to another if the cache is read before an authorization check runs — caching is only safe when it sits *behind*, not in front of, the authorization decision. |
| Elasticsearch (analytics store) | `03-elasticsearch-vs-sql-server.md` | See §3.3 below — this is the sharpest, most concrete instance of the problem, named explicitly per this document's scope. |
| Kafka consumers | `05-kafka-comparison.md`, `20-outbox-pattern.md` | A consumer that materializes data into a read model (Elasticsearch or otherwise) on behalf of a request context needs to either preserve the original caller/ownership metadata through the pipeline or re-derive it — it cannot assume "this data reached me, so it's fine to serve to whoever asks." |

The unifying risk: **each of these is a place a query can be issued against stored data.** v1 had exactly one such place (the relational database, behind one repository interface). v2 has at least three (sharded relational, Redis, Elasticsearch), and every one of them is a place where "did we check this caller is allowed to see this data" can be silently skipped if the check isn't enforced somewhere that *every* query path is guaranteed to pass through.

### 3.3 The concrete case: does AF-10's analytics-read API correctly scope Elasticsearch queries?

Per `fn-analytics.md` §1.1/Q25 (cross-referenced, not restated): AF-10's analytics-read API is **creator-only** — "the only consumer is the link creator, via the same API." In v1, that scoping is straightforward: the analytics query runs against SQLite/the relational store, filtered by a `ShortUrlId` that's already implicitly owned by the requesting creator because v1's whole data model and query surface is small and singular.

`03-elasticsearch-vs-sql-server.md` moves the click/analytics event store to Elasticsearch for exactly the aggregation-query reasons that document lays out (its own scope, not restated here). **This is the honest question this document exists to ask: once analytics data lives in Elasticsearch instead of the relational store, does AF-10's creator-only scoping still hold, or was it only holding by accident of v1's simpler topology?**

- **Elasticsearch does not inherit the relational database's row-level assumptions.** A relational query built through a repository/EF Core layer that already has an implicit `WHERE OwnerId = @caller`-shaped convention baked into how `nfr-security.md`/`fn-analytics.md`'s design was built is a different code path, with different tooling, than a query built against an Elasticsearch index using the ES query DSL. **There is no default "row-level security" carried over automatically** — an Elasticsearch `terms`/`date_histogram` aggregation query (`03-elasticsearch-vs-sql-server.md` §... — the exact aggregation shape that document recommends) that omits a `term` filter on `CreatedBy`/`OwnerDepartmentId` will happily return aggregate click data across **every** creator's links, not just the requesting one — because nothing about Elasticsearch's index structure enforces ownership; it only returns what the query asks for.
- **This is a realistic, not hypothetical, way to introduce a data leak:** a developer building the AF-10 analytics endpoint against the new Elasticsearch backend, focused on getting the aggregation query shape right (the hard, novel part of that document's design), can straightforwardly ship a query that's correct in its aggregation logic and wrong in its authorization scope — because nothing in the query itself fails, errors, or looks obviously incomplete if the ownership filter is missing. It is exactly the kind of gap that a single-database, single-query-path v1 system structurally could not have, and a multi-store v2 system structurally can, unless it's designed against deliberately.

### 3.4 Where enforcement must live — recommendation

**Recommendation: centralize authorization checks in the `Application` layer, before any data-store query is issued — relational or Elasticsearch — and never rely on a downstream data store's own access control as the sole enforcement point.**

- **Concretely:** the AF-10 analytics use case (`Application` layer, per the layering `design-guidelines.md` already establishes) resolves and validates "is `currentUser` authorized to see analytics for `shortUrlId`/`creatorId`" as its own explicit step, **before** constructing the Elasticsearch query — and the resulting authorized scope (a specific `creatorId`, or a specific set of `shortUrlId`s the caller owns) is what's passed down to become a mandatory filter clause in the query itself, not an afterthought applied to the query's results after the fact. The same pattern applies to any sharded-relational-store query (`10-database-partitioning-sharding.md`) and any Redis-cached read (`07-redis-caching-and-invalidation.md`) — the authorization decision is made once, at the top of the use case, and its output is a constraint every downstream query construction must honor, not a check re-derived independently (and therefore riskily re-implementable-incorrectly) at each store.
- **Why not "let Elasticsearch enforce it" (e.g., via index-level or document-level security features):** Elasticsearch does have document-level/field-level security capabilities (available in some licensing tiers) that could theoretically enforce this at the store level. This document does not recommend relying on that as the *sole* control, for the same reason defense-in-depth is generally preferred over a single enforcement point: if that configuration is ever missing, misconfigured, or bypassed by a different code path querying the same index (e.g., an internal admin/debugging tool, or a future second consumer of the same Elasticsearch cluster), there is no second check to catch the gap. Application-layer enforcement, by contrast, is enforced regardless of which store-level features are or aren't configured, and is visible in one place (the use case) for review — consistent with `nfr-security.md` §3's own reasoning for why input validation lives in the `Application` layer rather than trusting DTO attributes alone ("the validator is the authoritative boundary... not a substitute").
- **This does not mean store-level access controls are worthless** — enabling them where available is still good defense-in-depth practice. It means they must never be the *only* place the check happens, because a downstream store's access control is invisible to, and unverifiable from, the `Application`-layer code that's supposed to be the system's actual authorization boundary.
- **This recommendation generalizes beyond AF-10.** Every future read path this v2 architecture adds (a department-scoped link listing, per `10-database-partitioning-sharding.md` §5's cross-shard-query discussion; any future BI/reporting surface, explicitly out of scope today per `fn-analytics.md` Q24 but a plausible future addition) inherits the same rule: authorization is resolved once, in `Application`, before the query is constructed against whichever store answers it.

## 4. Explicitly Out of Scope — Documented Exceptions, Not Silent Omissions

Consistent with `nfr-security.md`'s own convention (its §2.3, §11) of naming scope boundaries rather than letting them be discovered by absence, this document draws its own boundary explicitly:

- **Full penetration testing.** Nothing in this document substitutes for an actual pen test against a real deployment. The threats and mitigations here are architectural design decisions, not the output of an adversarial test against running infrastructure.
- **WAF configuration specifics.** §2.1 and §2.3 both name a WAF/bot-management layer as part of a complete answer to distributed scanning and DDoS, but this document does not specify rules, thresholds, or a specific product's configuration — that is deployment/operations work, downstream of this design.
- **Formal security certification** (SOC 2, ISO 27001, or similar). `nfr-security.md` §6 already scopes formal GDPR/CCPA compliance out of v1 for the same reason; this document extends that same scope boundary — nothing here is written against, or claims to satisfy, a certification framework's control set.
- **A concrete, chosen authentication/authorization mechanism.** `nfr-security.md` §10.2's mocked-auth exception is not resolved by this document. §2.2 and §3 both describe requirements a *future* real mechanism must satisfy (stuffing protection at the credential-verification step; consistent enforcement across every v2 data store) — this document specifies constraints on that future design, it does not build it.
- **Threat-intel/reputation-feed vendor selection.** §1's re-scanning and domain-monitoring mitigations assume *some* automated reputation-check capability exists (the same one `nfr-security.md` §4.2 already leaves as an Infrastructure-layer implementation detail) — this document does not pin down which vendor/feed, consistent with that existing decision.
- **A specific anomaly-detection implementation for §2.1's aggregate-scan-pattern gap.** This document names the gap and the category of signal (aggregate 404-rate spikes not attributable to a single IP) but does not design the detection pipeline itself — that is flagged as a genuine open item, not solved here.

## 5. Summary of Positions

| # | Concern | Position taken here | Traces to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Domain reputation risk at scale | A constant false-negative rate produces a large absolute daily volume of uncaught bad links at 5M creates/day; mitigated by continuous re-scanning (tiered by click volume/age), an automated fast-takedown path propagated through every cache layer, and domain-reputation standing as a monitored operational metric | §1; `nfr-security.md` §4.3; `06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md`; `13-observability-at-scale.md` |
| 2 | Distributed short-code enumeration | Per-IP limits (`12-distributed-rate-limiting.md`) are necessary but insufficient against a scan spread across many IPs; aggregate anomaly detection (e.g., 404-rate spikes) is a named, unsolved gap | §2.1; `12-distributed-rate-limiting.md` |
| 3 | Credential/API-key stuffing | Out of scope until a real auth mechanism is chosen (`nfr-security.md` §10.2); this document states the requirement that mechanism must satisfy, not a design | §2.2 |
| 4 | DDoS against redirect | The CDN layer (`06-output-caching-bff-cdn.md`) is the primary absorption layer, stated explicitly; origin rate limiting is the second line of defense | §2.3 |

**This document does not claim to make the shortener secure against a determined, well-resourced attacker** — it names three specific gaps extreme scale opens up beyond `nfr-security.md`'s v1 baseline, and gives concrete, honest positions on each, consistent with every other document in this series.
